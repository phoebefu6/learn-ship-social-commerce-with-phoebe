Data-quality tests that fail loudly

A schema says what a row should look like. A test says what happens when a row does not. Serai Commerce, the constructed enabler, receives three marketplace exports, and on the raw files four simple checks find 333 rows that would quietly bend every number above them. Tonight you write those four checks, give each one a repair rule, and wire them into a gate that stops the pipeline instead of printing a warning nobody reads.

Part 0

Where the tests sit

Session 1 built the merged schema: five tables, each with a declared grain, and an order-level fact table that adds up to the cent. This session is the second half of the data engineering layer. Every sentence you wrote at the top of a table ("one row per order") becomes a test, and every test gets a decision written next to it: stop, repair, quarantine or flag. Serai Commerce is constructed, and so are the defects planted in its raw exports (re-sent orders, orphan customer keys, refund lines); the orders underneath are real derived Olist rows, and the timestamp inversions are real too. Serai's identity gate and its crosswalk come from the Social Commerce Enablers course, Analyst session 3; this session uses the crosswalk and does not rebuild it.

Part 1 · covers the four checks and pass rates per export

Four checks per export

6 min live

Four questions cover most of what goes wrong in commerce exports. Is every order key unique? Does every customer key point at a real customer? Do the timestamps run in order? Is every amount zero or more? Ask them per export, because a pass rate you cannot split by source tells you there is a problem, not where it lives.

LiveThe four checks, one sentence each3 min▶
CheckThe sentence it testsGrain it runs atBlocking?
Keys uniqueOne row per order, after the key is trimmed and lower-casedorder rowYes

Timestamps orderedPurchase, then approval, then carrier pickup, then delivery, all presentorder rowNo, a flag

Each check comes straight from a sentence session 1 wrote at the top of a table. That is the point: a grain you declared but never tested is a hope. Notice the last column. Three checks protect the counts every later layer depends on, so they block. The timestamp check catches something real but harmless to the metrics, so it flags. Part 2 explains why that difference is a decision, not a technicality.

LiveReading the grid by export, not by total2 min▶

Export C is the smallest file, 1,823 rows, and the dirtiest: 96.93% of its keys unique and only 94.73% of its customer keys resolving. Export B is the only one carrying refund lines, so its amounts check passes at 98.16% while A and C sit at 100.00%. Summed across all three, the keys check would read a respectable-looking 98.27% (152 failing of 8,766), which hides the fact that one source is re-sending orders at more than three times the rate of another. When the alert says "Export C, customer key, 96 rows", the person who owns the Export C integration knows it is theirs before they open a notebook.

Self-studyNormalise before you test for uniqueness2 min▶

Serai's re-sent orders arrive under a variant of the original key: extra whitespace, different case, a re-send suffix. Test uniqueness on the raw string and the re-send passes, because the two keys differ by a character. Test it after the key rule from session 1's mapping contract (strip, lower-case, drop the re-send suffix) and the duplicate surfaces. The general rule: a uniqueness test is only as strong as the normalisation in front of it, so run the key rule first and keep the raw key beside the normalised one for the audit trail.

Real world

The timestamp column is not planted. Every other defect in Serai's raw exports is constructed for teaching. The timestamp failures are not: in the real Olist rows, 122 of Serai's 8,614 orders show the carrier picking up the parcel before the payment was approved, and 1 has no approval time at all. On the full public file the build-along below counts 1,350 delivered orders with the same inversion. Real data carries real oddities, and a test suite has to say what it does with each one.

Part 2 · covers the repair rule for each failing check

Repair, quarantine or flag

6 min live

A failing row is not one problem. A re-sent order should vanish, an orphan key can be fixed, a refund line belongs in a different table, and an odd timestamp on a real delivery should stay exactly where it is. Write the action next to the check, so the repair is as reviewable as the test.

LiveThe repair rule, written beside each check3 min▶
Failing rowActionWhy that actionSerai raw exports
Re-sent order (key repeats after normalising)Drop, log the dropped keyIt is the same order twice; keeping it doubles GMV and invents a same-day repeat customer152 dropped
Customer key not in the customer tableResolve through the crosswalkThe order is real; only the pointer is broken, and the identity gate knows the right person113 resolved
Negative line (a refund posted as a line)Quarantine to an adjustments tableRefunds are real money but not item value; mixing them into lines corrupts GMV both ways64 quarantined
Timestamps out of order, or one missingFlag and keepA real delivery with an odd log; dropping it deletes a real order and moves every count122 + 1 flagged

Read the third column out loud in review. If nobody can say why a row is dropped rather than repaired, the rule is not ready. A crosswalk you cannot resolve through (a key the identity gate has never seen) is not a repair case at all: it becomes a quarantine, and the count goes in the run report.

LiveWhy timestamp inversions are flagged, not dropped2 min▶

A carrier scan logged before payment approval is almost certainly a logging artefact: two systems with different clocks, or an approval written after the fact. The parcel still shipped and arrived, the customer still paid, and the review score still exists. Drop those 122 orders and Serai's order count, GMV and repeat rate all move for a reason that has nothing to do with the business. Keep them with a flag and they change no metric, while anyone measuring approval-to-pickup time can filter them out on purpose. The rule of thumb: drop only what you can prove is not a real event; flag what is real but odd.

Self-studyThe quarantine table is a deliverable, not a bin2 min▶

Give the adjustments table the same care as the fact table: a grain (one row per adjustment line), the source export, the original order key, and the date it was quarantined. Finance will ask for refunds by month, and the answer should come from this table rather than from somebody re-reading raw files. A quarantine nobody can query is a delete with extra steps. The same goes for the drop log: when an integration owner disputes a dropped re-send, the logged key is the evidence.

Real world

Multiple reviews per order, kept on purpose. In Serai's sample, 39 orders carry more than one review row and 68 carry none. Neither is an error to drop: some customers really review twice, and many never answer the survey Olist sends after delivery. Session 1 kept every review row in the schema and chose the first by answer time for the fact table. The same instinct applies to every check here: know which oddities are the world being messy and which are the pipeline being wrong.

Part 3 · covers the gate that stops the pipeline

Fail loudly

5 min live

A check that only warns has already decided the question for you: it ships the bad rows. The registry, the model and the gen AI layer all read whatever the data engineering layer hands them, so the honest move is a gate that raises an error, names the export and the check, and stops the run until the repair is done.

LiveBlocking and flagging, as code3 min▶

Two lists, decided in advance. Blocking checks raise an error that stops the run and prints every failing check with its count and export. Flag checks write their rows to a flags table and let the run continue. The gate never decides which list a check belongs to at run time; a human decided that in review, with the reason written down.

★ The gate, in one functionBLOCKING = ["keys unique", "customer key resolves", "amounts >= 0"] FLAG = ["timestamps ordered"] def gate(results): # results: {check: {export: failing_rows}} failed = {c: n for c in BLOCKING for e, n in results[c].items() if n} if failed: raise DataQualityError(f"blocking failures, stop the run: {failed}") write_flags(results, FLAG) # kept rows, queryable later

The block above is the shape, not a runnable script; the build-along writes the runnable version against the real file.

LiveWhat warn-and-continue costs, layer by layer2 min▶

Serai's bench can skip the data engineering layer and let every later layer read the raw exports. The registry reads a repeat rate of 4.61% instead of 3.00%, and its agreement with the reference falls to 65.14% on that metric, because every re-sent order looks like a second purchase. The gen AI layer drops from 15 to 7 of 20 outputs passing, with agreement down to 50%, because review summaries quote the true review count while the raw frame says Store 2 has 155 reviews instead of 153. The model is the honest surprise: lift moves only from 3.71x to 3.30x, because re-sends mostly add noise to its label. With 24 positives in the scoring month, one hit moves lift by 0.41, so treat that gap as roughly one order. Damage is uneven, which is exactly why a warning feels safe and is not.

Self-studyA stop must reach a person2 min▶

A pipeline that stops in silence is no better than one that warns in silence. The failure message should name the export, the check, the count and the first few failing keys, and it should reach whoever owns that export's integration. Keep yesterday's good tables in place while the run is stopped, so dashboards show slightly stale but correct numbers rather than fresh wrong ones, and say on the dashboard that the data is held. Contract-style testing, orchestration and alert routing at depth belong to the DataOps course, Builder session 3 there; this session stops at the four checks and the gate.

Real world

Why the registry cannot catch a re-send later. Olist's real rows contain genuine same-day repeat orders: in Serai's sample, 83 of the 197 orders followed by another within 90 days were followed on the same day. A re-sent order looks exactly like one of those once it reaches the analytics layer: same customer, same day, a second order key. Downstream, nobody can tell them apart. The only place the difference is visible is the key check at the gate, which is why it blocks rather than warns. Session 4 comes back to the real same-day repeats.

Build-along

The four checks and the gate, in pandas

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Use the same download as session 1, kagglehub.dataset_download("olistbr/brazilian-ecommerce"), and run each block from a scratch folder. The outputs in the comments are what these exact blocks print on the full public file (pandas 2.1). Serai's bench runs the same four checks on its seeded 8,614-order sample with planted defects, which is why its numbers differ from these.

Load the files and keep delivered orders. Point d at the downloaded folder. Parse all four timestamps on read so the order check compares times, not strings.

★ Block 1 · loadimport pandas as pd d = "path/to/olist/" ts = ["order_purchase_timestamp", "order_approved_at", "order_delivered_carrier_date", "order_delivered_customer_date"] orders = pd.read_csv(d + "olist_orders_dataset.csv", parse_dates=ts) items = pd.read_csv(d + "olist_order_items_dataset.csv") pays = pd.read_csv(d + "olist_order_payments_dataset.csv") cust = pd.read_csv(d + "olist_customers_dataset.csv") o = orders[orders.order_status == "delivered"] li = items[items.order_id.isin(o.order_id)] pa = pays[pays.order_id.isin(o.order_id)] print("orders", len(o), "lines", len(li), "payments", len(pa)) # prints: orders 96478 lines 110197 payments 100756

Write the three blocking checks so each returns the failing rows. A boolean per row, True for a failure, means the same function feeds the pass rate, the count and the list of keys to investigate.

★ Block 2 · blocking checksdef keys_unique(o): return o.order_id.duplicated() # True = a failing row def key_resolves(o, cust): return ~o.customer_id.isin(cust.customer_id) def amounts_non_negative(li, pa): return pd.concat([li.price < 0, li.freight_value < 0, pa.payment_value < 0]) for name, fails in [("keys unique", keys_unique(o)), ("customer key resolves", key_resolves(o, cust)), ("amounts >= 0", amounts_non_negative(li, pa))]: print(f"{name:22} pass {1 - fails.mean():.2%} fail {fails.sum()}") # prints: keys unique pass 100.00% fail 0 # customer key resolves pass 100.00% fail 0 # amounts >= 0 pass 100.00% fail 0

On Serai's exports you would normalise the key first (session 1's key rule) and run each check grouped by source_export. The public file has one source and clean keys, so every blocking check passes here.

Write the timestamp check as a flag. Break it into its parts so the printout says which step is out of order, then keep the flagged order keys in their own table.

★ Block 3 · the flag checkp, a, c, dl = (o[col] for col in ts) inverted = (a < p) | (c < a) | (dl < c) missing = o[ts].isna().any(axis=1) print("approval before purchase", (a < p).sum(), "| carrier before approval", (c < a).sum(), "| delivery before carrier", (dl < c).sum(), "| a timestamp missing", missing.sum()) flags = o.loc[inverted | missing, ["order_id"]].assign(flag="timestamp order") print("flagged and kept", len(flags), "of", len(o)) # prints: approval before purchase 0 | carrier before approval 1350 | delivery before carrier 23 | a timestamp missing 23 # flagged and kept 1396 of 96478

Carrier before approval is the same real inversion Serai's sample shows 122 times. None of these orders leaves o; the flags table only points at them.

Wire the blocking checks into a gate that raises. The error message carries every failing check and its count, so whoever reads it knows where to look.

★ Block 4 · the gateclass DataQualityError(Exception): pass def gate(o, li, pa, cust): counts = {"keys unique": keys_unique(o).sum(), "customer key resolves": key_resolves(o, cust).sum(), "amounts >= 0": amounts_non_negative(li, pa).sum()} failed = {k: int(v) for k, v in counts.items() if v} if failed: raise DataQualityError(f"blocking failures {failed}") return "all blocking checks pass" print(gate(o, li, pa, cust)) # prints: all blocking checks pass

Break it on purpose. Plant three re-sent orders and two refund lines, the same two defect classes Serai's exports carry, and watch the gate stop the run.

★ Block 5 · plant two defectso_bad = pd.concat([o, o.head(3)]) # three re-sent orders li_bad = li.copy() li_bad.loc[li_bad.index[:2], "price"] *= -1 # two refund lines try: gate(o_bad, li_bad, pa, cust) except DataQualityError as e: print("pipeline stopped:", e) # prints: pipeline stopped: blocking failures {'keys unique': 3, 'amounts >= 0': 2}

In a real pipeline you would not catch the error: you would let it end the run. The try is only here so the notebook keeps going.

The check that looks finished and protects nothing if o.order_id.duplicated().any(): print("warning: duplicate order keys") # the run carries on with them

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why are Serai's 122 timestamp inversions flagged and kept rather than dropped?

Drop only what you can prove is not a real event. A carrier scan logged before approval is a logging oddity on a real delivery, so it is flagged, kept, and filterable by anyone who needs clean timing.

2 · The raw exports have 333 blocking failures, but the repair drops 152, resolves 113 and quarantines 64, which sums to 329. Where did the other 4 go?

The customer key check fails on 117 rows: 113 originals plus 4 re-sent copies of the same orders. Dropping a re-send removes its key failure too, so only 113 keys need resolving. Counting failures and counting fixes are different things; reconcile them in the run report.

3 · A colleague proposes logging a warning on duplicate order keys instead of stopping the run. What does the bench say that costs Serai?

The model is the layer that survives (lift 3.71x to 3.30x, about one hit of 24), which is what makes a warning feel safe. The registry and the gen AI facts take the damage, and once a re-send is past the gate it looks exactly like a real same-day repeat.
