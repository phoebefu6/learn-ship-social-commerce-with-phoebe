A repeat-purchase model with a time split

Serai Commerce, the constructed enabler running on 8,614 real derived Olist orders, wants to know which of this month's buyers will come back within 90 days, so its brands can spend a follow-up budget on the likeliest ones. Tonight you build that model the honest way: seven features that exist at the moment of purchase, a label window that closes before the cutoff, and one score that counts, the month the model has never seen. You also see what a random split would have told you instead, and why you should not believe it.

Part 1 · covers the seven features and the one left out

Features as of purchase

6 min live

A model is asked its question at one moment. For Serai, that moment is the purchase: the order lands, the score is computed, the brand decides whether to follow up. Every feature must be a value that existed at that moment. If a column is filled in later, it does not belong, however predictive it looks.

LiveThe seven features, and what each one is standing in for4 min▶
FeatureAs computedThe behaviour it proxies
prior orderslog(1 + orders this person placed before this one)habit: people who came back once come back again
recency1 / (1 + days since the prior order), 0 for a first ordermomentum: a recent second order is a warmer signal
order valuelog(1 + item value in BRL)commitment, with the log taming a long tail
freight sharefreight / (freight + item value)friction: heavy shipping cost per item discourages a return
instalmentsthe most instalments on any payment rowprice sensitivity and payment habit
voucher1 if any payment row is a voucherpromotion-driven buying
itemslines in the orderbasket breadth

Every one of them is computed from the order itself and from the same person's earlier orders. None needs a timestamp later than the purchase. The bench fits them as a logistic regression by full-batch gradient descent: features standardised, weights starting from zero, learning rate 1.0, 1,000 steps, an L2 term of 0.001. Starting from zero with a fixed step count means there is no random seed in the optimiser at all; the only seed in the chain is the sample draw that produced Serai's 8,614 orders. Rerun it and you get the same weights to the last digit.

LiveWhy review score is left out, even though it is right there2 min▶

Review score sits in the order-level fact table from session 1, one column over from instalments, and it is tempting. But Olist's dataset notes say the satisfaction survey goes out once the customer receives the product or the estimated delivery date is due. At the moment Serai wants to score an order, the review does not exist yet. A model trained with it would be graded on information it could never have at prediction time.

Serai's sample makes it concrete: 83 of the 197 repeat orders within 90 days were placed on the same day as the order before them. For those customers the next order happened before any review could have been written. Session 6 hunts this kind of timing trap properly; tonight the rule is simply "stamped at order time, or it waits".

Real world

The survey timing is in the data documentation, not in the columns. Nothing in Olist's review table flags the score as post-delivery: it is a tidy integer from 1 to 5 next to an order id. The timing lives in a sentence of the dataset description. That is the normal state of commerce data, and it is why a feature list needs a "time known" column written by someone who read the documentation, not inferred from the schema.

Part 2 · covers the time split and the label window

The label window closes before the cutoff

6 min live

The label asks about the 90 days after each order. So a training order is only usable once its 90 days have fully passed. If month T ends on 2018-01-31, the last order you may train on is from 2017-11-02. Train on anything later and some labels are still open: a customer who will come back in February is recorded as "did not return", and the model learns from wrong answers.

LiveThree dates, and the arithmetic between them3 min▶

Start from the cutoff and work backwards. The cutoff is the last day of data you would have if you were running the model for real: 2018-01-31, the end of month T. The label needs 90 days of future, so the latest order whose label is complete is 90 days before that: 2017-11-02. That gives 2,744 training orders, of which 60 were followed by another order from the same person within 90 days. Scoring happens on month T+1, February 2018: 574 orders, 24 of which turned out to be repeats, a base rate of 4.18%.

★ The split, as three constantsCUTOFF = "2018-01-31" # end of month T: the last day you may look at LABEL_DAYS = 90 TRAIN_END = "2017-11-02" # CUTOFF minus LABEL_DAYS: every label closed SCORE_MONTH = ("2018-02-01", "2018-02-28") # month T+1, never seen in training

Write them as constants at the top of the script, derived from each other, so nobody moves one without the other.

LiveWhy a random split would flatter the model3 min▶

A random 80/20 split shuffles all orders and holds out a fifth. Three things then go wrong at once. Orders from the scoring period sit in training, so the model learns that period's habits before it is asked about them. The same person's earlier and later orders land on both sides, so the model has effectively met the customer it is scoring. And training rows near the end carry labels that look into the very weeks the test rows come from. None of this raises an error. It just makes the number better than anything the model will see in production.

scikit-learn's documentation for TimeSeriesSplit names the problem directly: it "provides train/test indices to split time-ordered data, where other cross-validation methods are inappropriate, as they would lead to training on future data and evaluating on past data." In the build-along you run both splits on the full public file and see the gap for yourself.

Self-studyWalk-forward folds with TimeSeriesSplit3 min▶

One time split gives one number. To see how stable it is, walk forward: train on the first block, test on the next, then train on the first two, test on the third, and so on. scikit-learn's TimeSeriesSplit does exactly that, and its documentation notes that "successive training sets are supersets of those that come before them." One thing it does not know about is your label window. Each fold's training set must still stop 90 days before its test block starts, or the open-label problem returns inside every fold. Step 7 of the build-along adds that trim by hand.

Real world

The library that ships shuffling by default also ships the warning. scikit-learn is the most common place a first model gets built, and its general-purpose splitters shuffle or ignore order. The same library documents TimeSeriesSplit with a plain statement that other cross-validation methods are inappropriate for time-ordered data. The tool will not choose for you; the date column has to.

Part 3 · covers AUC, lift and reading a small sample

AUC and lift, read honestly

5 min live

AUC answers "does the model rank repeat buyers above one-time buyers?" Lift answers the question a brand actually pays for: "if I follow up only with the top tenth, how many more repeat buyers do I reach than by picking at random?" On month T+1 the bench prints AUC 0.7894 and top-decile lift 3.71x. Both are real. Both rest on 24 positives.

LiveAUC in one sentence, lift in one sentence2 min▶

AUC. Pick one repeat buyer and one non-repeat buyer at random; AUC is the chance the model scores the repeat buyer higher. 0.5 is a coin, 1.0 is perfect. scikit-learn's roc_auc_score computes it "from prediction scores", which is why you pass probabilities, not 0/1 predictions. Month T+1 reads 0.7894.

Lift at the top decile. Sort February's 574 orders by score, take the top 58, count the repeats: 9. Random picking would find 4.18% of 58, about 2.4. Nine divided by that is a lift of 3.71x. A brand following up with those 58 customers reaches more than three and a half times as many future repeat buyers as one following up with 58 customers picked at random.

LiveTwenty-four positives, and what that does to every number2 min▶

Month T+1 has 24 repeat buyers. The top decile found 9 of them. Had it found 8, lift would read 3.30x; had it found 10, 4.12x. Each hit is worth 0.41 of lift. That is the honest width of the number, and it should be said out loud whenever the 3.71x is shown. The same small-sample noise explains the odd ordering of the two AUCs: training AUC is 0.6248 on 2,744 orders with only 60 positives, a weak and noisy label, so it is below the test AUC by chance, not because the model got better on data it had never seen.

Self-studyWhat scikit-learn's logistic regression does that a statistics textbook does not2 min▶

scikit-learn's linear-models guide describes logistic regression as "implemented as a linear model for classification rather than regression", and adds: "Regularization is applied by default, which is common in machine learning but not in statistics." The bench mirrors that with a small L2 term of 0.001 on standardised features. The practical consequence: the weights are shrunk a little toward zero, so they are not the unbiased coefficients a statistics package would print, and you should not read them as effect sizes. They are good for ranking, which is the job.

Real world

The bigger file disagrees, and that is the lesson. Run the same seven features on the full public file in the build-along, with scikit-learn's default solver, and month T+1 (February 2018, 6,555 orders, 216 repeats) prints AUC 0.5907 and lift 2.13x, not 0.7894 and 3.71x. Same data source, same features, same split rule, a larger sample and a different optimiser. Neither number is wrong. The bench's 24 positives make its figure wide; the full file's 216 make its figure narrower and lower. Report the sample size beside every AUC you show.

Build-along

The model in scikit-learn, time split first

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Download the public dataset with kagglehub.dataset_download("olistbr/brazilian-ecommerce") (no login needed) and run each block in order from a scratch folder. The printed lines below are what the script prints on the full public file, run with pandas and scikit-learn as written. The bench works on Serai's seeded 8,614-order sample with its own optimiser, so these numbers differ from the bench's, and that difference is part of the lesson.

Load and roll everything up to one row per order. The session 1 rule holds: roll up first, then join. One delivered order has no payment row and drops out of the inner join, which is why the count is 96,477 rather than session 1's 96,478.

★ Block 1 · load and roll upimport numpy as np import pandas as pd from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, TimeSeriesSplit d = "path/to/olist/" orders = pd.read_csv(d + "olist_orders_dataset.csv", parse_dates=["order_purchase_timestamp"]) items = pd.read_csv(d + "olist_order_items_dataset.csv") pays = pd.read_csv(d + "olist_order_payments_dataset.csv") cust = pd.read_csv(d + "olist_customers_dataset.csv") o = orders[orders.order_status == "delivered"].merge(cust[["customer_id", "customer_unique_id"]], on="customer_id") lines_o = items.groupby("order_id").agg(n_lines=("order_item_id", "size"), price=("price", "sum"), freight=("freight_value", "sum")) pays_o = pays.groupby("order_id").agg(inst=("payment_installments", "max"), voucher=("payment_type", lambda s: int((s == "voucher").any()))) f = o.set_index("order_id").join(lines_o, how="inner").join(pays_o, how="inner").reset_index() f["day"] = f.order_purchase_timestamp.dt.normalize() f = f.sort_values(["customer_unique_id", "order_purchase_timestamp", "order_id"]).reset_index(drop=True) print("orders", len(f)) # prints: orders 96477

Build the seven features and the label. Every feature looks only at this order and the same person's earlier rows (cumcount, shift(1)). The label is the only thing that looks forward (shift(-1)), and it is the target, never an input.

★ Block 2 · features as of purchase, label 90 days aheadg = f.groupby("customer_unique_id") f["prior"] = g.cumcount() gap = (f.day - g.day.shift(1)).dt.days f["recency"] = np.where(f.prior > 0, 1 / (1 + gap), 0.0) nxt = (g.day.shift(-1) - f.day).dt.days f["y"] = (nxt <= 90).astype(int) # another order within 90 days: the target f["log_prior"] = np.log1p(f.prior) f["log_value"] = np.log1p(f.price) f["freight_share"] = f.freight / (f.freight + f.price) FEATS = ["log_prior", "recency", "log_value", "freight_share", "inst", "voucher", "n_lines"] print("positives", f.y.sum(), f"base rate {f.y.mean():.2%}") # prints: positives 2149 base rate 2.23%

Split by time, with the label window closed. Train on orders up to 2017-11-02 so every label has its full 90 days by the 2018-01-31 cutoff; score February 2018.

Fit and read AUC on both sides. The scaler sits inside the pipeline so its means and spreads come from training rows only. Pass probabilities, not class labels, to roc_auc_score.

★ Block 4 · fit, then AUCmodel = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)) model.fit(train[FEATS], train.y) p_tr = model.predict_proba(train[FEATS])[:, 1] p_te = model.predict_proba(test[FEATS])[:, 1] print(f"train AUC {roc_auc_score(train.y, p_tr):.4f} month T+1 AUC {roc_auc_score(test.y, p_te):.4f}") # prints: train AUC 0.6257 month T+1 AUC 0.5907

Compute top-decile lift. This is the number a brand budget is set against. Sort with a stable sort so ties break the same way every run.

★ Block 5 · lift at the top deciledef lift_top_decile(y, p): order = np.argsort(-p, kind="stable") n = int(np.ceil(len(y) * 0.10)) hits = int(np.asarray(y)[order[:n]].sum()) return n, hits, (hits / n) / np.mean(y) n, hits, lift = lift_top_decile(test.y.values, p_te) print(f"top decile {n} orders, {hits} repeats, lift {lift:.2f}x") # prints: top decile 656 orders, 46 repeats, lift 2.13x

Now do it the flattering way, once, on purpose. Shuffle the same period into a random 80/20 split and score it. Seeing the better number appear is what makes the time-split habit stick.

★ Block 6 · the random split, for comparison onlyrtr, rte = train_test_split(f[f.day <= "2018-02-28"], test_size=0.2, random_state=20260929) m2 = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)).fit(rtr[FEATS], rtr.y) p2 = m2.predict_proba(rte[FEATS])[:, 1] n2, h2, l2 = lift_top_decile(rte.y.values, p2) print(f"random split AUC {roc_auc_score(rte.y, p2):.4f} lift {l2:.2f}x") # prints: random split AUC 0.6249 lift 2.41x

Walk forward with TimeSeriesSplit, trimming each fold's training rows to closed labels. Four folds, four numbers: the spread tells you how much to trust any single one.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Review score predicts repeat purchase reasonably well. Why is it left out of the feature list?

Olist sends the survey once the product arrives or the estimate is due. At scoring time it is not there, and 83 of 197 repeat orders were placed the same day, before any review could exist. A feature must exist at the moment of prediction.

2 · Month T ends on 2018-01-31 and the label is "another order within 90 days". Why does training stop at 2017-11-02?

2018-01-31 minus 90 days is 2017-11-02. Every training order up to then has its full window observed by the cutoff. Train later and some labels are wrong answers. TimeSeriesSplit does not know about your label window; you add the gap yourself.

3 · Month T+1 has 24 positives and the top 58 orders hold 9 of them: lift 3.71x. What is the honest way to report it?

Nine hits out of 24 is a small count. Saying how much one hit moves the number is the honest width. And train AUC below test AUC here is small-sample noise in a weak label, not evidence of a good model.
