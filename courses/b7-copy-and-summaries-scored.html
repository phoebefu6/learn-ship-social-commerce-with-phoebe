Listing copy and review summaries, scored

A language model will happily write a listing that says "best", a summary that says customers "love" a store, and a review count it never looked up. Serai Commerce, the constructed enabler, wants draft copy for its sellers, and it will only ship drafts that pass a check. Tonight you write that check as code: three tests every draft must pass, facts read from the rows rather than typed in, and a hand-labelled golden set that tells you when the check itself is wrong.

Part 0

Where the gen AI layer sits

Sessions 1 to 6 gave Serai a schema, its tests, a metric registry, cohorts and one repeat-purchase model. Sessions 7 and 8 are the gen AI layer. Tonight is the part that can be fully automated: scoring draft listing copy and draft review summaries before a seller ever sees them. Two things are honest up front. First, no language model runs here: Serai's 20 drafts (10 listings, 10 store summaries) are pre-written and constructed, written by hand to behave the way model drafts behave. Second, the facts they talk about are real: product category and weight from the Olist products table, and review counts, mean score and the share of one- and two-star reviews recomputed from real review SCORES. No review text is read anywhere, because the Olist data is licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 and Serai ships scores only. Eval method in depth, from sampling a golden set to judging with a second model, belongs to Learn AI Evals with Phoebe; tonight applies it to seller copy.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A Python rubric that scores any draft against three checks (required facts present, banned claims absent, length in range), a facts function that reads those required facts from one-row-per-order data, and a golden-set check that reports agreement, false passes and false fails. You run it on five drafts you write yourself, then rerun it on un-repaired rows and watch a correct summary fail.

Part 1 · covers the three checks and why they are code

The rubric as code

6 min live

A rubric is a function: it takes a draft and the facts for the thing the draft describes, and it returns pass or fail with a reason. Writing it as code, not as a checklist a reviewer keeps in their head, is what lets it run on every draft, every day, with the same answer twice.

LiveThe three checks, and what each one is for3 min▶
CheckWhat it testsWhat it catchesWhat it cannot catch
Required facts presentEvery fact for this listing or store appears in the text, in the exact form the rubric expectsA count the model guessed, a mean it rounded wrong, a fact it left outA true fact in a different format; a false fact the rubric was never told to check
Banned claims absentNo match for a list of patterns: superlatives, guarantees, "free shipping", authenticity and certification claims, absolutes, and sentiment wordsClaims a seller cannot back with data, and words like "love" that review scores cannot showA new phrasing of the same claim that no pattern matches
Length in rangeWord count: 25 to 70 for a listing, 20 to 60 for a summaryRambling drafts and one-line stubsAnything about content

On Serai's 20 constructed drafts the rubric passes 15. Two listings fail on banned claims (L2 says "best" and "free shipping"; L6 says "authentic" and "guaranteed"), one summary fails on "love" (S3), one summary states a review count the rows do not support (S5), and one listing, L4, fails for a reason Part 3 unpacks. The banned list, the length limits and the 70% pass gate are Serai's constructed policy, not an industry standard. Write your own from your marketplace's listing rules and your legal team's list of claims you cannot substantiate.

LiveWhy "love" is banned in a summary built from scores2 min▶

A review score is a number from 1 to 5. It tells you how a buyer rated an order, not why, and not how they feel about the store. A summary that says customers "love" a store is making a sentiment claim the data cannot support, however high the mean. Serai's summaries are written from scores only, because the review text cannot be shipped under the licence, so the rubric bans the words that would pretend otherwise. The general rule: a generated claim may be no stronger than the evidence the generator was given.

Self-studyRegex patterns are a floor, not a ceiling3 min▶

Word-boundary patterns such as \bbest\b catch the obvious cases cheaply and never drift. They also miss "unbeatable", "top-rated" and every phrasing nobody thought of. That is acceptable for a first gate as long as you treat the pattern list as a living file: every time a reviewer rejects a draft the rubric passed, the reason becomes a new pattern or a new required fact. Part 3's golden set is how you find those reasons systematically rather than by complaint.

★ Four of Serai's ten banned patternsBANNED = { "superlative": r"\bbest\b", "guarantee": r"\bguarantee(d|s)?\b", "free shipping": r"\bfree shipping", "sentiment scores cannot show": r"\b(love|loves|loved|rave|raves)\b", }
Real world

The security community already names this failure. OWASP's Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 lists LLM09 Misinformation, which "occurs when LLMs produce false or misleading information that appears credible". Among its prevention measures is automatic validation of key outputs. A rubric that checks every draft before it ships is exactly that: the cheapest validation there is, run on every output rather than on a sample.

Part 2 · covers required facts recomputed from the data

Facts come from rows

6 min live

The required-fact check is only as good as the facts it compares against. Serai's rubric never stores "Store 2 has 153 reviews" as a constant. It recomputes the count, the mean and the low-score share from the order rows every time it runs. That is the right design, and it means a defect in the rows becomes a defect in the gen AI layer.

LiveStore 2, clean and raw3 min▶

On the repaired frame from session 2, Store 2 has 153 reviewed orders, a mean score of 3.9 and 18% of scores at one or two stars. Draft S1 says exactly that, and passes. Skip the data engineering layer and run the same rubric on the raw exports, where 152 re-sent orders are still sitting under variant keys: Store 2 now reads 155 reviews. S1 has not changed a word, and it fails its required-fact check. Across all 20 drafts the pass count falls from 15 to 7, agreement with the golden verdict falls from 90% to 50%, and 9 drafts that a careful reviewer would publish now fail. Every one of the ten summaries is affected; the listings, whose facts come from the products table rather than from order rows, are not.

LiveThe failure that would not show up in production2 min▶

Serai's drafts were written from the true facts, so dirty rows make good drafts fail, loudly. In production the direction is usually reversed and much quieter: the model is fed facts from the same dirty rows the rubric checks against, the draft says "155 reviews", the rubric recomputes 155, and the draft passes. Both sides agree and both are wrong. That is why the gen AI layer sits on top of the data engineering layer and not beside it. A rubric is a consistency check between text and facts; it cannot tell you the facts are true. Only the tests in session 2 can.

Self-studyWhich review counts as the order's review3 min▶

A "review count" sounds unambiguous until you read the rows. The fact function counts reviewed orders: one score per order, the first by answer time, as the fact table from session 1 keeps it. Change that choice (last review, mean of all reviews, every review row) and the required facts change with it, and every summary written to the old definition fails. Put the choice in the metric registry from session 3, next to the other definitions, so the copy layer and the analytics layer cannot disagree about what "153 reviews" means.

Real world

Olist's review rows do not line up one to one with orders. In Serai's 8,614-order sample, 39 orders carry more than one review and 68 carry none; on the full public file it is 525 and 646. Olist also documents that the satisfaction survey goes out when the customer receives the product or when the estimated delivery date is due, so a score can exist for an order that has not yet arrived. Any summary that quotes a count is quoting a definition. Make it the registry's definition, not the model's guess.

Part 3 · covers the golden set, false passes and false fails

Checking the checker

5 min live

A rubric that passes 15 of 20 drafts tells you nothing until you know whether it passed the right 15. The golden set answers that: a careful person labels every draft "fit to publish" or not, by hand, and you measure how often the rubric agrees. Serai's gate asks for at least 70% of drafts passing and at least 85% agreement with the golden verdict, both constructed policy.

LiveThe false pass and the false fail3 min▶

Of Serai's 20 drafts, the rubric and the golden labels agree on 18: 14 drafts both call fit, 4 both call unfit (L2, L6, S3 and S5). Two disagree, and they disagree in opposite directions.

  • L4, the false fail. The draft gives the weight as 1.75 kg. That is correct: the products table holds 1,750 grams. The required-fact check looks for the string "1750 g", does not find it, and fails a draft a reviewer would publish. A false fail costs a seller a good draft and teaches people to ignore the rubric.
  • L8, the false pass. The draft says the watch ships from Sao Paulo. The listing's seller is in Rio de Janeiro state. The rubric checks category and weight for listings, not origin, so the draft passes with a false claim in it. A false pass is the expensive one: it ships.

The right response to both is to fix the rubric, never the threshold. Normalise units before matching, and add the seller's state to the required facts for listings. Then rerun the golden set to confirm the fixes did not create new disagreements.

LiveWhy 20 labels is a smoke test, not a measurement2 min▶

Each draft is 5 points of agreement. One more disagreement drops Serai from 90% to 85%, exactly on the gate; two more drop it below. That is the same small-number honesty as session 5's model, where one hit of 24 moves lift by 0.41. Twenty labels are enough to find the two kinds of rubric gap on this page. They are not enough to promise a seller a false-pass rate. Grow the golden set as you grow the catalogue, sample it so it covers every category and store size, and keep the labeller blind to the rubric's verdict. How to size and sample a golden set properly is Learn AI Evals with Phoebe's territory.

Self-studyDoes scored copy sell more? The evidence disagrees4 min▶

A rubric tells you a draft is safe to publish, not that it will sell. On whether AI-written listing copy sells more, the evidence points in different directions, and it is worth reading both sides before anyone promises a seller an uplift.

  • Randomized platform experiments. Fang, Yuan, Zhang, Donati and Sarvary ran field experiments on a cross-border e-commerce platform; the abstract (read at source) reports effects "ranging from no detectable impact to 16.3%" depending on gen AI's marginal contribution to the workflow, with gains mainly through conversion. Earlier versions and summaries reported about +2% for AI product descriptions, with the 16.3% belonging to a pre-sale chatbot workflow, not to listing copy; versions differ, and that split is reported, not read in full.
  • Survey experiments on mentioning AI. Cicek, Gursoy and Lu (2024), reported via a Washington State University press release, found in experimental surveys of more than 1,000 US adults that products described as using artificial intelligence were less popular, more so for high-risk products. That is about MENTIONING AI in the copy, not about copy that AI wrote.

Different questions, settings and outcomes. Neither settles whether Serai's sellers would sell more with scored drafts. The professional move is to measure it on your own catalogue with a controlled test, and to quote no uplift as a forecast until you have.

Real world

NIST has a word for L8. NIST's Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1) defines confabulation as "the production of confidently stated but erroneous or false content". L8 is a textbook case: a fluent, specific, plausible shipping claim that no row supports. The rubric did not invent it, but the rubric let it through, which is why a golden set reviewed by a person sits on top of every automatic check.

Build-along

The rubric in Python, on drafts you write

★ 22 min · everyone builds

One script, five blocks, pandas and the standard library. Every draft, fact and golden label in it is constructed: you write them, so you know the right answer before the rubric gives its own. The printed outputs below are what the script prints as written, on Python 3.9 with pandas 2.1.4. Change a draft and your numbers will differ, which is the point.

Write the policy as data. Six banned patterns and two length ranges, a subset of Serai's constructed policy. Keep them in one place the rubric reads, so adding a pattern is a one-line change.

★ Block 1 · the policyimport re import pandas as pd BANNED = { "superlative": r"\bbest\b", "guarantee": r"\bguarantee(d|s)?\b", "free shipping": r"\bfree shipping", "authenticity claim": r"\b(original|authentic|genuine)\b", "sentiment scores cannot show": r"\b(love|loves|loved|rave|raves)\b", "absolute claim": r"\b(always|never|everyone)\b", } LENGTH = {"listing": (25, 70), "summary": (20, 60)}

Compute the facts from rows, never type them in. Ten constructed review scores for one constructed store, then the same frame with one order re-sent. The clean function counts one row per order; the raw one counts every row, the way a rubric does when the data engineering layer is skipped.

★ Block 2 · facts from rowsscores = pd.DataFrame({ "order_key": ["o1", "o2", "o3", "o4", "o5", "o6", "o7", "o8", "o9", "o10"], "score": [5, 4, 5, 1, 4, 5, 3, 2, 5, 4], }) resent = pd.concat([scores, scores[scores.order_key == "o4"]]) # one order re-sent def store_facts(df): one = df.drop_duplicates("order_key") n = len(one) return {"reviews": f"{n} reviews", "mean": f"{one.score.mean():.1f}", "low": f"{round((one.score <= 2).mean() * 100)}%"} def raw_facts(df): n = len(df) return {"reviews": f"{n} reviews", "mean": f"{df.score.mean():.1f}", "low": f"{round((df.score <= 2).mean() * 100)}%"} clean = store_facts(resent) dirty = raw_facts(resent) print("clean", clean) print("dirty", dirty) # prints: clean {'reviews': '10 reviews', 'mean': '3.8', 'low': '20%'} # dirty {'reviews': '11 reviews', 'mean': '3.5', 'low': '27%'}

Write the rubric as one function and five drafts by hand. D1 and D3 are what you would publish. D2 breaks the banned list, D4 claims sentiment the scores cannot show, and D5 invents a same-day shipping promise the rubric has no fact for. Each draft carries your golden label: True means fit to publish.

★ Block 3 · the rubric and five constructed draftsdef score(text, kind, facts): low = text.lower() missing = [v for v in facts.values() if v.lower() not in low] banned = [k for k, rx in BANNED.items() if re.search(rx, text, re.I)] lo, hi = LENGTH[kind] words = len(text.split()) ok = not missing and not banned and lo <= words <= hi return ok, missing, banned, words listing_facts = {"category": "desk lamps", "weight": "780 g"} # constructed outputs = [ # (id, kind, facts, golden, text) ("D1", "listing", listing_facts, True, "A steady reading light for a small desk. This desk lamps item weighs 780 g, so it stays put when you " "adjust the arm. Check the plug type and cable length in the photos before you order."), ("D2", "listing", listing_facts, False, "The best desk lamp you will ever own, guaranteed. At 780 g it feels solid, and every buyer in the " "desk lamps range says the same thing about the warm light it gives."), ("D3", "summary", "store", True, "Across 10 reviews this store averages 3.8 out of 5. About 20% of scores are one or two stars, so the " "low tail is worth a look before you scale its listings."), ("D4", "summary", "store", False, "Customers love this store. Across 10 reviews it averages 3.8, and 20% of scores are one or two " "stars, which nobody should worry about."), ("D5", "listing", listing_facts, False, "A compact light for late work. This desk lamps item weighs 780 g and ships the same day from our " "own warehouse, so most buyers have it within two days. The photos show the arm reach."), ] def run(store): rows = [] for oid, kind, facts, golden, text in outputs: f = store if facts == "store" else facts ok, missing, banned, words = score(text, kind, f) rows.append({"id": oid, "rubric": ok, "golden": golden, "words": words, "missing": missing, "banned": banned}) return pd.DataFrame(rows) res = run(clean) print(res.to_string(index=False)) # prints: # id rubric golden words missing banned # D1 True True 37 [] [] # D2 False False 33 [] [superlative, guarantee] # D3 True True 32 [] [] # D4 False False 24 [] [sentiment scores cannot show] # D5 True False 36 [] []

Check the checker. Three numbers: agreement, false passes, false fails. D5 is your L8: a plausible claim about delivery that no fact in the rubric covers.

★ Block 4 · agreement with your golden labelsagree = (res.rubric == res.golden).mean() false_pass = (res.rubric & ~res.golden).sum() false_fail = (~res.rubric & res.golden).sum() print(f"pass {res.rubric.sum()} of {len(res)} agreement {agree:.0%} false pass {false_pass} false fail {false_fail}") # prints: pass 3 of 5 agreement 80% false pass 1 false fail 0

Rerun the same drafts on the raw rows. Nothing about the copy changes. D3, a draft you would publish, now fails because the facts it is checked against counted the re-send.

★ Block 5 · the same drafts, dirty factsres2 = run(dirty) print(res2[["id", "rubric", "golden", "missing"]].to_string(index=False)) print(f"pass {res2.rubric.sum()} of {len(res2)} agreement {(res2.rubric == res2.golden).mean():.0%}") # prints: # id rubric golden missing # D1 True True [] # D2 False False [] # D3 False True [11 reviews, 3.5, 27%] # D4 False False [11 reviews, 3.5, 27%] # D5 True False [] # pass 2 of 5 agreement 60%

Close the gap you found. Add a required "ships" fact to listing_facts (for example the seller's state) and a banned pattern for delivery promises such as same day. Rerun blocks 3 and 4 and confirm D5 fails and nothing else moves. That edit, made after reading a disagreement, is the whole loop.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 7 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Draft S1 says Store 2 has 153 reviews, mean 3.9, 18% one or two stars. On repaired rows it passes; on raw exports it fails. What changed?

The copy is identical. Required facts are recomputed from the rows on every run, so a re-sent order inflates the count and a correct summary fails. Across all 20 drafts that takes the pass count from 15 to 7.

2 · L8 says the watch ships from Sao Paulo; the seller is in Rio de Janeiro state. The rubric passes it. What is the right fix?

A false pass means the rubric never checks the fact that was wrong. Fix the rubric, not the threshold: add the missing fact, then confirm against the golden labels that nothing else moved.

3 · A product owner reads "AI-written listings lift sales" in a headline and asks you to put an uplift in Serai's forecast. What does the evidence support?

Fang et al. report effects from no detectable impact to 16.3% depending on the workflow, and the 16.3% is reported to belong to a chatbot, not listing copy. Cicek et al., reported, is about mentioning AI in copy. Different questions; neither is a forecast for Serai.
